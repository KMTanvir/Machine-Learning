Module 21

Principal Component Analysis

When predictors are numerous and correlated, much of the information they carry is redundant. Principal component analysis finds a small set of new variables, built as linear combinations of the originals, that retain as much of the variation as possible. It is the foundation for dimension reduction, for visualisation, and for the regression methods of Module 22.

Learning Objectives
  • State the optimisation problem that defines the first principal component
  • Obtain principal components from the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix
  • Compute loadings, scores and the proportion of variance explained by hand
  • Decide whether to standardise the predictors, and explain why it matters
  • Choose the number of components using a scree plot and cumulative variance
  • Recognise what PCA cannot do
Unsupervised by construction PCA never looks at a response variable. It summarises the predictors alone, which makes it useful for exploration and visualisation, but also creates the weakness that Module 22 addresses: the directions of greatest variance in $X$ need not be the directions most useful for predicting $Y$.
Motivation

Why Reduce Dimension?

Three distinct problems with large $p$
  • Variance. Least squares with $p$ predictors estimates $p+1$ parameters. As $p$ approaches $n$ the fit becomes unstable, and when $p > n$ it is not unique at all.
  • Redundancy. Highly correlated predictors carry overlapping information. Ten predictors that are near copies of one another supply little more than one predictor does, yet cost ten degrees of freedom.
  • Visualisation. Data in $p$ dimensions cannot be plotted directly. There are $\binom{p}{2}$ pairwise scatterplots, which is $190$ plots for $p = 20$, and no pair shows the joint structure.

The idea

Replace the $p$ original predictors by $M \ll p$ derived variables $Z_1, \ldots, Z_M$, each a linear combination of the originals:

$$Z_m = \sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{jm}X_j$$

The weights $\phi_{jm}$ are called loadings, and the resulting values of $Z_m$ for each observation are called scores. PCA chooses the loadings so that the first few components capture as much of the variance in $X$ as possible.

A projection, not a selection Subset selection in Module 16 discarded predictors. PCA keeps all of them but combines them, so every original variable still contributes. That preserves information but costs interpretability: a component is a weighted blend rather than a single measurable quantity.
Definition

The First Principal Component

The optimisation problem Assume each $X_j$ has been centred to mean zero. The first principal component is the normalised linear combination with the largest sample variance: $$\max_{\phi_{11},\ldots,\phi_{p1}} \; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{j1}x_{ij}\right)^{2} \qquad \text{subject to} \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{j1}^{2} = 1$$

The constraint is essential. Without it the objective could be made arbitrarily large simply by scaling the loadings up, so the problem would have no solution. Fixing the loading vector to unit length makes the comparison one of direction rather than magnitude.

Subsequent components

The second principal component maximises the same variance subject to being uncorrelated with the first, which for centred data means the loading vectors are orthogonal:

$$\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{j1}\phi_{j2} = 0$$

Continuing in this way produces at most $\min(n-1,\,p)$ components, mutually orthogonal and ordered by decreasing variance.

Computation

Solving PCA by Eigendecomposition

The last slide asked for the direction of greatest variance. Linear algebra gives a ready made answer: look at the covariance matrix of the data and find its eigenvectors.

The recipe
  1. Centre the predictors and compute the $p \times p$ sample covariance matrix $\mathbf{S}$.
  2. Find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors: $$\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\phi}_m = \lambda_m \boldsymbol{\phi}_m, \qquad \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_p \geq 0$$
  3. Each eigenvector $\boldsymbol{\phi}_m$ is a set of loadings, a direction in the data.
  4. Its eigenvalue $\lambda_m$ is the variance of the component along that direction.
The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is the first principal component, the next largest is the second, and so on.
Eigen quantityWhat it means for PCA
Eigenvector $\boldsymbol{\phi}_m$The direction of the $m$th component (its loadings)
Eigenvalue $\lambda_m$How much variance the data has along that direction, $\operatorname{Var}(Z_m) = \lambda_m$
Two facts worth knowing
  • The components are uncorrelated. Because $\mathbf{S}$ is symmetric, its eigenvectors are perpendicular to one another.
  • No variance is lost. The eigenvalues add up to the total variance of the original variables: $$\sum_{m=1}^{p}\lambda_m = \sum_{j=1}^{p}\operatorname{Var}(X_j)$$ PCA only redistributes the same total across new directions, concentrating it in the first few.
Software usually uses the singular value decomposition instead of forming $\mathbf{S}$, which is more stable numerically, but the answer is the same.
Worked Example

Worked Example: PCA on Two Variables

Two centred predictors have sample covariance matrix

$$\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$
Step 1: the characteristic equation $$\det(\mathbf{S} - \lambda\mathbf{I}) = (5-\lambda)(2-\lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = 0$$ Note $7 = \operatorname{trace}(\mathbf{S})$ and $6 = \det(\mathbf{S}) = 10 - 4$. Factorising, $$(\lambda - 6)(\lambda - 1) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_1 = 6, \quad \lambda_2 = 1$$
Step 2: the loading vectors For $\lambda_1 = 6$, solve $(\mathbf{S}-6\mathbf{I})\boldsymbol{\phi} = \mathbf{0}$: $$\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_1 \\ \phi_2\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{0} \;\Longrightarrow\; \phi_1 = 2\phi_2$$ Any multiple of $(2,1)$ works; normalising to unit length, $$\boldsymbol{\phi}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.8944\\0.4472\end{pmatrix}$$ For $\lambda_2 = 1$, solve $4\phi_1 + 2\phi_2 = 0$, giving $\phi_2 = -2\phi_1$ and $$\boldsymbol{\phi}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.4472\\-0.8944\end{pmatrix}$$
Step 3: checks that should always be made
CheckVerification
Eigenvalue equation$\mathbf{S}(2,1)^{\top} = (12,6)^{\top} = 6(2,1)^{\top}$  ✓
Trace preserved$\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 6+1 = 7 = 5+2$  ✓
Determinant preserved$\lambda_1\lambda_2 = 6 = \det(\mathbf{S})$  ✓
Orthogonality$\boldsymbol{\phi}_1^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_2 = \tfrac{1}{5}(2 - 2) = 0$  ✓
Step 4: interpretation The scores are $Z_1 = 0.8944X_1 + 0.4472X_2$ and $Z_2 = 0.4472X_1 - 0.8944X_2$. Both loadings on $Z_1$ are positive, so the first component is a weighted average, an overall size measure, with $X_1$ receiving twice the weight of $X_2$ because it is the more variable. The second component contrasts the two variables. The proportion of variance explained is $$\text{PVE}_1 = \frac{6}{7} = 0.8571, \qquad \text{PVE}_2 = \frac{1}{7} = 0.1429$$ so a single component retains $85.7\%$ of the total variance, and the correlation between the original variables, $2/\sqrt{10} = 0.6325$, is what makes this possible.
Variance Explained

Proportion of Variance Explained and the Scree Plot

Definitions $$\text{PVE}_m = \frac{\lambda_m}{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\lambda_k}, \qquad \text{Cumulative PVE} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_k}{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\lambda_k}$$ Each $\text{PVE}_m$ lies in $[0,1]$ and they sum to one across all $p$ components.

Reading the plot Three criteria point the same way here. The elbow in the scree plot occurs after the third component, where the curve flattens. The Kaiser rule, keep components with $\lambda_m > 1$ when working from the correlation matrix, also retains three. A cumulative threshold of $90\%$ is first exceeded at three components, which retain $95\%$. Reducing from five variables to three loses only $5\%$ of the variance.
These rules are conventions, not tests None has a formal justification and they frequently disagree. The elbow is often ambiguous, and the Kaiser rule is known to over retain. When the components feed a supervised model, the honest criterion is cross validated prediction error, which is exactly the approach taken in Module 22.
Scaling

Why Scaling Changes Everything

PCA is not scale invariant The components maximise variance, and variance depends on units. Measuring a length in millimetres rather than metres multiplies its variance by $10^6$, so that variable would dominate the first component for no substantive reason.

A concrete illustration

Suppose income is recorded in taka, with standard deviation $50{,}000$, and years of education has standard deviation $3$. The variances are $2.5\times 10^{9}$ and $9$. The first principal component of the raw covariance matrix would be essentially income alone, since it accounts for more than $99.99\%$ of the total variance. Education could not influence the answer whatever its correlation structure.

The remedy Standardise each predictor to unit variance before applying PCA, which is equivalent to performing the eigendecomposition on the correlation matrix rather than the covariance matrix. Every variable then contributes variance $1$, so the total variance is $p$ and no variable is privileged by its units.
SituationUseReason
Variables in different unitsCorrelation matrixUnits are arbitrary, so variance comparisons are meaningless
Variables in the same units, comparable spreadEither, often covarianceDifferences in variance are genuine information
Some variables far more variable, deliberatelyCovariance matrixYou want the dominant variables to dominate
Practical default Standardise unless there is a clear reason not to. Note also that when working from the correlation matrix the eigenvalues sum to $p$, which is what makes the Kaiser rule of $\lambda_m > 1$ meaningful: a component is retained only if it explains more variance than a single original variable does.
Interpretation

Loadings, Scores and Biplots

A biplot superimposes the two, plotting observations as points in the space of the first two components and variables as arrows given by their loadings.

Reading a biplot

  • Arrows pointing in a similar direction indicate positively correlated variables.
  • Arrows at roughly right angles indicate approximately uncorrelated variables.
  • Arrows in opposite directions indicate negative correlation.
  • A long arrow means the variable is well represented by the first two components; a short one means it lies mostly in the discarded directions, so its position should not be over interpreted.
  • Observations far from the origin are extreme on those components, and points clustering together are similar across all the variables at once.
Signs are arbitrary If $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ is an eigenvector then so is $-\boldsymbol{\phi}$, with the same eigenvalue. Flipping the sign reflects the plot but changes nothing substantive, so two software packages can produce mirror image biplots from identical data. Never interpret the sign of a loading on its own; only the relative signs within a component carry meaning.
Limitations

What PCA Cannot Do

It ignores the response PCA is unsupervised. The direction of greatest variance in $X$ carries no guarantee of being related to $Y$. It is entirely possible for the response to depend on the last principal component, in which case discarding low variance directions destroys exactly the signal that mattered. This is the motivation for partial least squares in Module 22.
It only finds linear structure Components are linear combinations, so PCA can only capture linear relationships. Data lying on a curved manifold, such as points arranged on a spiral or a circle, cannot be summarised by any single linear direction, and PCA will report that several components are needed when in truth one nonlinear coordinate would suffice.
It is sensitive to outliers Variance is a squared quantity, so a single extreme observation can pull the first component towards itself. Inspect the data, or use a robust variant, before trusting the directions.
Components can be hard to explain A component with substantial loadings on twenty variables is a mathematical object, not a measurable quantity. Gains in dimension come at a direct cost in interpretability, which is the opposite of the trade offered by lasso in Module 17, where the surviving predictors keep their original meaning.
When PCA is the right tool Predictors are numerous and genuinely correlated; the relationships are approximately linear; the aim is exploration, visualisation, or feeding a downstream model rather than explaining individual coefficients; and there is no strong reason to believe the response depends on a low variance direction.
In-Class Exercise

In-Class Exercise

Problem Two standardised predictors have correlation $0.6$, so the correlation matrix is $$\mathbf{R} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
  1. Find both eigenvalues.
  2. Find the corresponding unit loading vectors.
  3. Compute the proportion of variance explained by the first component.
  4. Explain what happens to the eigenvalues as the correlation approaches $1$, and as it approaches $0$.

1. $\det(\mathbf{R}-\lambda\mathbf{I}) = (1-\lambda)^2 - 0.36 = 0$, so $(1-\lambda) = \pm 0.6$ and $$\lambda_1 = 1.6, \qquad \lambda_2 = 0.4$$ Check: $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 2 = \operatorname{trace}(\mathbf{R}) = p$, as it must be for a correlation matrix.

2. For $\lambda_1 = 1.6$: $(1-1.6)\phi_1 + 0.6\phi_2 = 0$ gives $\phi_1 = \phi_2$, so $$\boldsymbol{\phi}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,\,1)^{\top} = (0.7071,\,0.7071)^{\top}$$ For $\lambda_2 = 0.4$: $0.6\phi_1 + 0.6\phi_2 = 0$ gives $\phi_2 = -\phi_1$, so $$\boldsymbol{\phi}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,\,-1)^{\top} = (0.7071,\,-0.7071)^{\top}$$ With two standardised variables the components are always the sum and the difference, whatever the correlation, because symmetry forces it.

3. $\text{PVE}_1 = 1.6/2 = \mathbf{0.80}$, so one component retains $80\%$ of the variance.

4. In general $\lambda_1 = 1+r$ and $\lambda_2 = 1-r$. As $r \to 1$ the eigenvalues tend to $2$ and $0$: the variables become identical, the data collapse onto a line, and one component captures everything. As $r \to 0$ both tend to $1$: the variables are uncorrelated, no direction is preferred, and PCA achieves no reduction at all. PCA is useful precisely to the extent that the predictors are correlated.

Problem 2 A study measures height in centimetres (variance $64$) and weight in kilograms (variance $144$), with covariance $72$.
  1. Find the first eigenvalue and its PVE using the covariance matrix.
  2. Height is now recorded in millimetres instead. Write the new covariance matrix.
  3. Without full calculation, explain what happens to the first component, and state what should be done instead.

1. $\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} 64 & 72 \\ 72 & 144\end{pmatrix}$, so $\operatorname{trace} = 208$ and $\det = 9216 - 5184 = 4032$. Then $$\lambda^2 - 208\lambda + 4032 = 0, \qquad \lambda = \frac{208 \pm \sqrt{208^2 - 4(4032)}}{2} = \frac{208 \pm \sqrt{27{,}136}}{2} = \frac{208 \pm 164.7301}{2}$$ giving $\lambda_1 = \mathbf{186.3650}$ and $\lambda_2 = 21.6350$. Hence $\text{PVE}_1 = 186.3650/208 = \mathbf{0.8960}$.

2. Multiplying height by $10$ multiplies its variance by $100$ and its covariance with weight by $10$: $$\mathbf{S}_{\text{new}} = \begin{pmatrix} 6400 & 720 \\ 720 & 144 \end{pmatrix}$$

3. Height now accounts for $6400$ of the total variance of $6544$, or $97.8\%$, so the first component becomes almost pure height and weight is effectively ignored. Nothing about the data changed, only the unit of measurement, which shows the result is an artefact. Standardise both variables and use the correlation matrix. Here $r = 72/\sqrt{64 \times 144} = 72/96 = 0.75$, giving $\lambda_1 = 1.75$ and $\text{PVE}_1 = 0.875$ whatever the units.

Problem 3 Explain why the loading vectors must be constrained to unit length, and why the second component is required to be orthogonal to the first. What would go wrong if each constraint were dropped?

Unit length. The variance of $Z = \sum\phi_j X_j$ scales with the squared length of $\boldsymbol{\phi}$: replacing $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ by $c\boldsymbol{\phi}$ multiplies the variance by $c^2$. Without the constraint the objective is unbounded, since taking $c \to \infty$ makes the variance arbitrarily large, and the maximisation has no solution. Fixing $\|\boldsymbol{\phi}\| = 1$ makes the problem about direction alone.

Orthogonality. Without it, the second component would simply be the first one again, since the same direction still maximises variance. Every subsequent component would be a copy, and nothing new would be learned. Requiring orthogonality forces each component into the subspace not yet explained, which is what makes the components carry non redundant information and what allows their variances to be added together in the PVE calculation.
