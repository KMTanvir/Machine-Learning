Module 17

Ridge Regression and Lasso

Regularisation (shrinkage) methods constrain or shrink coefficient estimates toward zero. This introduces bias but can substantially reduce variance, leading to lower test error. Unlike subset selection, regularisation methods fit the model to all predictors simultaneously and apply a smooth penalty.

Why regularise? OLS minimises RSS without any constraint on the size of coefficients. When predictors are correlated or $p$ is large relative to $n$, OLS coefficients are unstable (high variance). Regularisation shrinks coefficients, trading a small increase in bias for a large decrease in variance.

  • Understand the ridge shrinkage factor and its effect on correlated predictors
  • Derive the lasso and explain its sparsity-inducing property
  • Compare ridge and lasso through penalty structure, multicollinearity behaviour, and the bias-variance lens
  • Define the elastic net and describe when it is preferred
  • Select the regularisation parameter $\lambda$ using cross-validation

Ridge Regression

Ridge Regression

Ridge objective $$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\mathrm{ridge}} = \underset{\boldsymbol\beta}{\arg\min}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j x_{ij})^2 + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j^2\right\} = \underset{\boldsymbol\beta}{\arg\min}\bigl\{\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2\bigr\}$$ where $\lambda \geq 0$ is the regularisation parameter (tuning parameter). The intercept $\beta_0$ is not penalised.
Standardisation is essential. The ridge penalty treats all coefficients symmetrically. If predictors are on different scales, a large-scale predictor will have a small coefficient and a small-scale one a large coefficient, distorting the penalty. Always standardise predictors (subtract mean, divide by SD) before ridge regression.
Closed-form solution $$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\mathrm{ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$$ The matrix $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}$ is always invertible for $\lambda > 0$, even when $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is singular (i.e., even when $p \geq n$).
Ridge Properties

Properties of Ridge Regression

Shrinkage factor

For orthogonal predictors ($\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{I}$), the ridge estimate simplifies to:

$$\hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{ridge}} = \frac{\hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{OLS}}}{1 + \lambda}$$ Each OLS coefficient is shrunk toward zero by the same factor $1/(1+\lambda)$.
Bias Ridge introduces bias: $E[\hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{ridge}}] \neq \beta_j$ for $\lambda > 0$. The bias increases with $\lambda$.
Variance Ridge reduces variance: $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{ridge}}) < \mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{OLS}})$ for $\lambda > 0$. The variance decreases as $\lambda$ increases.
Ridge does not produce sparse solutions. Ridge shrinks all coefficients toward zero but never exactly to zero. All $p$ predictors remain in the model for any finite $\lambda$. Ridge is not useful for variable selection.
Correlated predictors Ridge handles multicollinearity well: the $\lambda\mathbf{I}$ term stabilises $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}$, which explodes when columns of $\mathbf{X}$ are nearly collinear. Ridge distributes the coefficient weight more evenly among correlated predictors.
The Lasso

The Lasso (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator)

Lasso objective $$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\mathrm{lasso}} = \underset{\boldsymbol\beta}{\arg\min}\left\{\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^p|\beta_j|\right\} = \underset{\boldsymbol\beta}{\arg\min}\bigl\{\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_1\bigr\}$$ The lasso uses an $\ell_1$ penalty on the coefficients.
Sparsity: the key property of lasso For sufficiently large $\lambda$, some lasso coefficients are set exactly to zero. The lasso simultaneously shrinks and selects predictors: the solution is sparse. This makes lasso interpretable and suitable for high-dimensional data.
No closed form Unlike ridge, the lasso has no closed-form solution in general (the $\ell_1$ penalty is not differentiable at zero). It is solved by coordinate descent or the LARS algorithm.

For orthogonal predictors, the lasso solution is soft-thresholding:

$$\hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{lasso}} = \mathrm{sign}(\hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{OLS}})\cdot\max\bigl(|\hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{OLS}}| - \lambda/2,\; 0\bigr)$$

The Kink

Why the Kink at Zero Matters

Each penalty is added on top of the RSS parabola (dashed blue). The shape of the penalty determines whether the combined minimum can land at $\beta=0$.

Ridge penalty: $\lambda\beta^2$ (smooth bowl)

$\lambda\beta^2$ is a smooth parabola with its minimum at $\beta=0$. Adding it to RSS produces a new smooth parabola. The combined minimum slides toward 0 as $\lambda$ grows but is never exactly there.

Lasso penalty: $\lambda|\beta|$ (V-shape, kink at 0)

$\lambda|\beta|$ is a V-shape with a sharp corner at $\beta=0$. Adding it to RSS creates a kink. Once $\lambda$ is large enough, this kink becomes the global minimum, setting $\hat\beta=0$ exactly.

The core difference $\beta^2$ is differentiable everywhere: its gradient at $\beta=0$ is 0, so it adds no force pulling the solution to exactly 0. $|\beta|$ is not differentiable at $\beta=0$: its subgradient includes 0, which means $\beta=0$ satisfies the optimality condition whenever the RSS gradient is smaller than $\lambda$. This is why lasso produces exact zeros and ridge does not.
Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity: Ridge Distributes, Lasso Picks Arbitrarily

Multicollinearity means two or more predictors are highly correlated. OLS breaks down: $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ becomes nearly singular and coefficient estimates explode with opposite signs, even when the true $\beta_1 \approx \beta_2$.

Numerical example: why OLS fails Suppose $n=10$, $r_{12}=0.95$, so $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} \approx \begin{pmatrix}10&9.5\\9.5&10\end{pmatrix}$ (det $= 9.75$, nearly singular). If $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix}21\\19\end{pmatrix}$ (tiny noise asymmetry), OLS gives $\hat\beta_1 \approx 3.03$, $\hat\beta_2 \approx -0.97$, wildly different from the true $\beta_1=\beta_2=1$. A small change in one observation flips the signs entirely.
Ridge: stable, shared coefficients

Adding $\lambda\mathbf{I}$ to $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ reduces the condition number. Both $\hat\beta_1$ and $\hat\beta_2$ converge toward equal, stable values as $\lambda$ grows: the effect of $X_1$ and $X_2$ is shared proportionally.

Lasso: arbitrary, unstable selection

With correlated predictors, many $(\beta_1,\beta_2)$ pairs give nearly identical RSS. Lasso picks the corner of the $\ell_1$ ball: one coefficient is zeroed out. Which predictor survives is arbitrary: it depends on tiny noise, not on which predictor is more informative.

Why ridge shares and lasso picks Ridge minimises a smooth objective: $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}$ always gives a unique, symmetric solution. When $X_1=X_2$ exactly, symmetry forces $\hat\beta_1^{\text{ridge}}=\hat\beta_2^{\text{ridge}}$. Lasso's $\ell_1$ ball has corners on the axes: identical predictors create a flat ridge in the RSS surface, and the $\ell_1$ penalty pushes the solution to any corner, picking one predictor and zeroing the other. The elastic net ($\alpha\|\beta\|_1+(1-\alpha)\|\beta\|_2^2$) solves this by combining both penalties.
Ridge vs Lasso

Ridge vs Lasso: When to Use Each

PropertyRidgeLasso
Penalty$\ell_2$: $\lambda\sum\beta_j^2$$\ell_1$: $\lambda\sum|\beta_j|$

Correlated predictorsDistributes weight evenlySelects one, ignores others
SolutionClosed formIterative (coordinate descent)
Works when $p \geq n$YesSelects at most $n$ predictors

Lasso and groups of correlated predictors When predictors are highly correlated, lasso tends to arbitrarily select one from each group and discard the rest. This can be unstable: small data perturbations change which predictor is selected. Ridge is more stable in this setting.
Elastic Net

Elastic Net

Elastic Net objective $$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\mathrm{EN}} = \underset{\boldsymbol\beta}{\arg\min}\left\{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 + \lambda\left(\alpha\sum_j|\beta_j| + \frac{1-\alpha}{2}\sum_j\beta_j^2\right)\right\}$$ where $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controls the mix: $\alpha=1$ gives lasso, $\alpha=0$ gives ridge.
Advantages over lasso
  • Groups correlated predictors together (ridge component)
  • Can select more than $n$ predictors
  • More stable than lasso under multicollinearity
Tuning Two hyperparameters: $\lambda$ (overall regularisation) and $\alpha$ (ridge/lasso mix). Typically fix $\alpha \in \{0.1, 0.5, 0.9\}$ via CV, then cross-validate over $\lambda$ on a grid.
Grouping effect (Zou and Hastie, 2005) The elastic net has the grouping property: when predictors are nearly identical, their elastic net coefficients are nearly equal. This is desirable when predictors represent different measures of the same underlying construct.
Selecting Lambda

Selecting the Regularisation Parameter

Cross-validation for $\lambda$
  1. Choose a grid of $\lambda$ values: e.g., $\lambda \in \{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100\}$ (log scale).
  2. For each $\lambda$: compute $k$-fold CV error.
  3. Select $\lambda^*$ that minimises CV error (or apply the one-SE rule for a sparser model).
  4. Refit the penalised model with $\lambda^*$ on the full training data.
Warm-start path Most software (R's glmnet, Python's sklearn) computes the full regularisation path from large to small $\lambda$ efficiently using warm starts: the solution at one $\lambda$ initialises the solver at the next. This is much faster than fitting each $\lambda$ independently.
Scale of $\lambda$ The value of $\lambda$ depends on the scale of $y$ and $X$. Always standardise predictors before fitting. After fitting, predictions and confidence intervals should be based on the unstandardised coefficients (transform back).
Worked Example

Worked Example: Ridge vs OLS

Simple case: $p = 1$, $n = 4$ observations. After standardising $X$: $X^\top X = 3.6$, $X^\top y = 7.2$. OLS: $\hat\beta_1^{\mathrm{OLS}} = 7.2/3.6 = 2.0$.

Ridge for $\lambda = 1.8$: $$\hat\beta_1^{\mathrm{ridge}} = \frac{X^\top y}{X^\top X + \lambda} = \frac{7.2}{3.6 + 1.8} = \frac{7.2}{5.4} = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.333$$ Shrinkage factor $= 1/(1 + \lambda/X^\top X) = 1/(1 + 0.5) = 2/3$. Ridge coefficient is $2/3$ of OLS coefficient.
Lasso soft-thresholding for $\lambda = 0.8$: $$\hat\beta_1^{\mathrm{lasso}} = \mathrm{sign}(2.0)\cdot\max(|2.0| - 0.8/2, 0) = 1 \times \max(1.6, 0) = 1.6$$
Summary: OLS $= 2.0$, Ridge $\approx 1.33$, Lasso $= 1.6$ (for these specific $\lambda$ values). Ridge shrinks more aggressively here because the ridge penalty acts on $\beta^2$; lasso subtracts a fixed threshold from $|\beta|$.
In-Class Exercise

In-Class Exercise

A standardised dataset has $n = 50$, $p = 2$ predictors with $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix}10 & 6\\6 & 10\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix}8\\4\end{pmatrix}$.

  1. Compute the OLS estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\mathrm{OLS}}$.
  2. Compute the ridge estimate for $\lambda = 4$.
  3. How much does $\hat\beta_1^{\mathrm{ridge}}$ differ from $\hat\beta_1^{\mathrm{OLS}}$? Explain the direction of the change.
  4. If the true data-generating process uses only $X_1$ (i.e., $\beta_2 = 0$), would lasso or ridge be preferable? Explain using the kink argument from Slide 6.
  5. In the Slide 5 visualisation, training RSS is always highest for $\lambda = 40$. Yet $\lambda = 40$ might give lower test MSE. Why does shrinking the slope below the OLS value hurt training RSS but can help test error?

1. $\det = 100-36=64$. $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1} = \frac{1}{64}\begin{pmatrix}10&-6\\-6&10\end{pmatrix}$. $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\mathrm{OLS}} = \frac{1}{64}\begin{pmatrix}56\\-8\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.875\\-0.125\end{pmatrix}$.

2. $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}+4\mathbf{I} = \begin{pmatrix}14&6\\6&14\end{pmatrix}$, $\det=160$. $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\mathrm{ridge}} = \frac{1}{160}\begin{pmatrix}14&-6\\-6&14\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}8\\4\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{160}\begin{pmatrix}88\\8\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.550\\0.050\end{pmatrix}$.

3. $\hat\beta_1$ fell from $0.875$ to $0.550$: a shrinkage of $0.325$ toward zero. Ridge always shrinks coefficients toward zero by adding $\lambda\mathbf{I}$ to the denominator matrix. The correlation ($r=0.6$) means the two coefficients adjust jointly, which is why $\hat\beta_2$ actually moved away from $-0.125$ toward $+0.050$.

4. Lasso is preferable. The lasso penalty $\lambda|\beta|$ has a kink at $\beta=0$ (Slide 6). When $\lambda$ is large enough, this kink becomes the global minimum of the combined objective, setting $\hat\beta_2=0$ exactly. Ridge uses $\lambda\beta^2$, which is smooth at 0 and never forces an exact zero, so ridge keeps a nonzero $\hat\beta_2$ even when $\beta_2$ is truly 0.

5. OLS minimises training RSS with no constraint: any other slope, including the shrunken ridge slope, produces strictly higher training RSS. However, OLS often overfits by chasing noise in the training data. The shrunken slope has more bias but much lower variance. When the variance reduction exceeds the bias increase, test MSE falls. This is the bias-variance tradeoff: ridge accepts worse training fit in exchange for a model that generalises better.

Practice Problems

Practice Problems

Problem 1: Conceptual
  1. As $\lambda \to \infty$ in ridge regression, what happens to all coefficients? What model does this correspond to?
  2. As $\lambda \to 0$ in lasso, what model does the lasso approach?
  3. Increasing $\lambda$ always increases training RSS. Explain why, and explain under what condition this leads to lower test MSE.

a. As $\lambda \to \infty$, all ridge coefficients $\hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{ridge}} \to 0$. The model approaches the null model (intercept only), predicting $\bar y$ for all observations. The model has maximum bias and zero variance (from regularisation).

b. As $\lambda \to 0$, the lasso penalty vanishes and the lasso solution approaches the OLS solution. When $\lambda = 0$, lasso is identical to OLS (with no constraint on the $\ell_1$ norm).

c. Training RSS always increases with $\lambda$ because OLS is the unique minimiser of RSS with no penalty: any constrained or penalised solution moves away from the RSS minimum. Test MSE decreases when the variance reduction from shrinkage exceeds the bias introduced. This occurs when OLS coefficients are noisy (small $n$, correlated predictors, or large $p$). When the true coefficients are large and well-estimated by OLS, shrinkage may not help.

Problem 2: Calculation

Orthogonal predictors. OLS estimates: $\hat\beta_1 = 3.0$, $\hat\beta_2 = 0.4$, $\hat\beta_3 = -1.8$.

  1. Compute ridge estimates for $\lambda = 2$ (with $X^\top X = nI$, $n=20$, so $X^\top X_{jj} = 20$).
  2. Compute lasso soft-threshold estimates for $\lambda = 1.5$. Show which coefficients are set to zero.
  3. Which estimator produces a sparser model? Does sparsity here correspond to the true model?

a. Ridge shrinkage factor $= n/(n+\lambda) = 20/22 \approx 0.909$. Estimates: $\hat\beta_1^R = 3.0 \times 0.909 \approx 2.727$, $\hat\beta_2^R = 0.4 \times 0.909 \approx 0.364$, $\hat\beta_3^R = -1.8 \times 0.909 \approx -1.636$. No coefficient is zero.

b. Soft threshold $= \lambda/2 = 0.75$. Apply: $\hat\beta_j^L = \mathrm{sign}(\hat\beta_j)\max(|\hat\beta_j|-0.75,\,0)$. $\hat\beta_1^L = \max(3.0-0.75,0) = 2.25$. $\hat\beta_2^L = \max(0.4-0.75,0) = 0$ (set to zero, since $0.4 < 0.75$). $\hat\beta_3^L = -\max(1.8-0.75,0) = -1.05$.

c. Lasso produces a sparser model: $\hat\beta_2 = 0$ is set to exactly zero, leaving a 2-predictor model. Ridge retains all 3. If $\beta_2 = 0$ in truth, then lasso has recovered the true sparse model and will have lower MSE than ridge (which keeps a spurious predictor). If $\beta_2 \neq 0$, lasso has introduced additional bias.

Problem 3: Conceptual
  1. A dataset has $n = 50$ and $p = 200$. OLS cannot be applied. Why? Which regularised method(s) can handle this setting?
  2. Two predictors $X_1$ and $X_2$ are perfectly correlated ($r = 1$). What does ridge do with their coefficients? What does lasso do?

a. When $p > n$, $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is rank-deficient (singular): its inverse does not exist and OLS has no unique solution. Ridge regression adds $\lambda\mathbf{I}$ to make the matrix invertible, so ridge works for any $\lambda > 0$. Lasso can also be applied (solved via coordinate descent) but selects at most $\min(n, p) = n = 50$ nonzero predictors. The elastic net combines both benefits and is often preferred when $p \gg n$.

b. Ridge: with $r=1$, $X_1$ and $X_2$ are interchangeable. Ridge distributes the coefficient weight equally between them: $\hat\beta_1^R = \hat\beta_2^R$ (by symmetry). Both stay nonzero and share the fitted effect. Lasso: arbitrary selection. Since both predictors are identical, lasso can achieve the minimum loss by putting all weight on either one (or any convex combination). In practice it will set one to zero and the other to a single value, but which one depends on the algorithm's initialisation. This instability is a known weakness of lasso under perfect multicollinearity.
