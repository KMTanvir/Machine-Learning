Module 22

Principal Components Regression and Partial Least Squares

Module 21 built components from the predictors alone. This module uses them for prediction, and then fixes the weakness that arises from ignoring the response. Both methods fit a regression in a low dimensional space of derived variables rather than in the original predictor space.

The common framework Both methods construct $M < p$ derived predictors $Z_1,\ldots,Z_M$ as linear combinations of the originals, then regress $Y$ on those: $$Z_m = \sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{jm}X_j, \qquad Y \approx \theta_0 + \sum_{m=1}^{M}\theta_m Z_m$$ They differ in one respect only: how the loadings $\phi_{jm}$ are chosen. PCR picks directions of maximum variance in $X$. PLS picks directions of maximum covariance with $Y$.
Why this reduces variance Fitting $M+1$ coefficients instead of $p+1$ constrains the model. That constraint introduces some bias, but sharply reduces variance, and a model with fewer, uncorrelated inputs is also easier to fit when $p$ is large relative to $n$.
PCR

Principal Components Regression

The algorithm
  1. Standardise each predictor to mean $0$ and variance $1$.
  2. Compute the principal components $Z_1,\ldots,Z_p$ of the predictors, as in Module 21.
  3. Keep the first $M$ components and fit least squares of $Y$ on $Z_1,\ldots,Z_M$.
  4. Choose $M$ by cross validation.

Recovering coefficients on the original scale

Because each $Z_m$ is a linear combination of the $X_j$, the fitted model can always be written back in terms of the original predictors:

$$\hat\beta_j^{\,\text{PCR}} = \sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat\theta_m \phi_{jm}$$

Every predictor therefore receives a non zero coefficient. PCR is a shrinkage method, not a selection method: it never sets a coefficient to exactly zero.

The constraint being imposed Using $M < p$ components restricts $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ to lie in the $M$ dimensional subspace spanned by the first $M$ loading vectors. The remaining $p-M$ directions are assigned a coefficient of exactly zero. PCR therefore applies a hard cut: it keeps some directions in full and discards the rest entirely.
Special cases With $M = p$ nothing is discarded and PCR reproduces ordinary least squares exactly, since the components merely form a rotated basis for the same space. With $M = 0$ the model is the intercept alone. So $M$ traverses the full range from the null model to OLS.
Worked Example

Worked Example: Recovering Coefficients on the Original Scale

Suppose there are three standardised predictors: $X_1$ (income), $X_2$ (education) and $X_3$ (experience). After running PCR we retain $M = 2$ components, whose loadings are

$$Z_1 = 0.6X_1 + 0.5X_2 + 0.4X_3, \qquad Z_2 = 0.2X_1 - 0.7X_2 + 0.6X_3$$

Regressing $Y$ on $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ gives the fitted model

$$\hat Y = 10 + 3Z_1 + 2Z_2, \qquad \hat\theta_1 = 3,\ \hat\theta_2 = 2$$
Step 1: recover the coefficient of each original predictor Use $\hat\beta_j = \sum_{m}\hat\theta_m\phi_{jm}$, that is, each predictor's loadings weighted by the component coefficients. $$\hat\beta_1 = 3(0.6) + 2(0.2) = 1.8 + 0.4 = 2.2$$ $$\hat\beta_2 = 3(0.5) + 2(-0.7) = 1.5 - 1.4 = 0.1$$ $$\hat\beta_3 = 3(0.4) + 2(0.6) = 1.2 + 1.2 = 2.4$$
Step 2: the model on the original scale $$\hat Y = 10 + 2.2X_1 + 0.1X_2 + 2.4X_3$$
Check with one observation Take $X_1 = X_2 = X_3 = 1$. Then $Z_1 = 0.6+0.5+0.4 = 1.5$ and $Z_2 = 0.2-0.7+0.6 = 0.1$, so through the components $\hat Y = 10 + 3(1.5) + 2(0.1) = 14.7$. Through the original predictors, $\hat Y = 10 + 2.2 + 0.1 + 2.4 = 14.7$. The two routes agree, as they must.
What to notice Only two coefficients $\hat\theta_1,\hat\theta_2$ were estimated, yet all three predictors receive a coefficient. This recovery step is identical for PCR and PLS; only the loadings $\phi_{jm}$ differ.
Why It Works

The Assumption Behind PCR

The key assumption PCR works well when the directions in which $X$ varies most are also the directions along which $Y$ varies most. Under that assumption the discarded low variance components carry little signal, so removing them costs almost no bias while removing a great deal of variance.

An example: predicting house prices

Suppose we predict house price from $X_1$ (house size), $X_2$ (number of bedrooms) and $X_3$ (distance from the city centre). Size and bedrooms move together, so most of the variation in $X$ lies along a single direction. PCR keeps the first two principal components and discards PC3.

1. The assumption is satisfied
PC1 (80% of the variance in $X$)Retained
Relationship with $Y$: very strong
PC2 (15% of the variance in $X$)Retained
Relationship with $Y$: moderate
PC3 (5% of the variance in $X$)Discarded
Relationship with $Y$: very weak
PCR discards only the component that says almost nothing about price. Little bias is added and the variance falls, so PCR works well.
2. The assumption fails
PC1 (80% of the variance in $X$)Retained
Relationship with $Y$: weak
PC2 (15% of the variance in $X$)Retained
Relationship with $Y$: moderate
PC3 (5% of the variance in $X$)Discarded
Relationship with $Y$: very strong
PCR discards the component that matters most for price, simply because it varies little in $X$. No choice of $M$ below $p$ recovers it.
Why PCR cannot tell the difference PCR ranks components by their variance in $X$ and never consults $Y$, so it treats both situations identically. That failure mode is the entire motivation for partial least squares.

PLS

Partial Least Squares

The first PLS direction Standardise all predictors and the response. Set each loading equal to the covariance between that predictor and the response: $$\phi_{j1} \;\propto\; \operatorname{Cov}(X_j,\,Y)$$ then normalise so that $\sum_j \phi_{j1}^2 = 1$. The first derived variable is $$Z_1 = \sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{j1}X_j$$ For standardised variables the covariance is the correlation, so PLS places the greatest weight on the predictors most strongly correlated with $Y$.

Subsequent directions

  1. Regress each $X_j$ on $Z_1$ and keep the residuals. This removes from every predictor the part already explained by the first direction.
  2. Compute $Z_2$ from those residuals in exactly the same way, using covariances with $Y$.
  3. Repeat until $M$ directions have been formed, choosing $M$ by cross validation.

This orthogonalisation step is what makes each new direction carry information the earlier ones did not.

Worked Example

Worked Example: Partial Least Squares with Two Components

Research problem: predict house price $Y$ from $X_1$ (house size), $X_2$ (number of bedrooms) and $X_3$ (distance from the city centre). There are five observations, and all variables are already standardised.

Step 1: the first PLS component Compute the covariance of each predictor with $Y$:
Predictor$X_1$$X_2$$X_3$
$\operatorname{Cov}(X_j, Y)$$3.600$$1.265$$1.549$
The weights are proportional to these covariances, normalised so that their squares sum to $1$: $$\phi_{11} = \frac{3.600}{\sqrt{3.600^2 + 1.265^2 + 1.549^2}} = \frac{3.600}{4.118} = 0.874, \qquad \phi_{21} = 0.307, \qquad \phi_{31} = 0.376$$ $$Z_1 = 0.874X_1 + 0.307X_2 + 0.376X_3$$ House size gets the greatest weight because it has the strongest covariance with price.
Step 2: remove the information captured by $Z_1$ Regress each predictor on $Z_1$ and keep the residuals: $$X_j = a_j + b_j Z_1 + r_j, \qquad r_j = X_j - \hat X_j, \qquad j = 1, 2, 3$$ This gives three residual predictors $r_1, r_2, r_3$, the parts of each $X_j$ that $Z_1$ did not explain.
Step 3: the second PLS component Repeat Step 1 using the residuals:
Residual$r_1$$r_2$$r_3$
$\operatorname{Cov}(r_j, Y)$$-0.173$$-0.553$$0.853$
Normalising gives $\boldsymbol\phi_2 = (-0.168,\,-0.536,\,0.827)$, so $$Z_2 = -0.168r_1 - 0.536r_2 + 0.827r_3$$
Step 4: the final regression Regress $Y$ on the two components, $Y = \theta_0 + \theta_1Z_1 + \theta_2Z_2 + \epsilon$. Suppose the estimated model is $$\hat Y = 0.925Z_1 + 0.165Z_2$$ The intercept is zero because the variables were standardised and centred.
Comparison

Choosing Between PCR and PLS

MethodUses $Y$?Sets coefficients to zero?Keeps original variables?Main strength
PCRNoNoNo, uses componentsHandles $p > n$; removes collinearity entirely

Practical guidance
  • Use PCR when $p$ is very large relative to $n$ and the predictors are highly collinear, since the components are exactly orthogonal by construction and the collinearity problem disappears.
  • Use PLS when a supervised reduction is wanted and few components must be retained, for example predicting a patient's blood pressure or disease risk from thousands of correlated gene expression measurements recorded for only a few hundred patients.
  • Always standardise before either method, and always choose the number of components $M$ by cross validation.
What the empirical evidence shows PLS is often expected to beat PCR because it is supervised, yet in practice the gain is small and inconsistent, since the extra variance from estimating directions using a noisy $Y$ offsets the reduction in bias. Choose on the basis of cross validated error and on which output you can defend to a reader.
In-Class Exercise

In-Class Exercise

Problem A PCR model with four standardised predictors retains $M = 2$ components, with loadings and fitted coefficients $$\boldsymbol\phi_1 = (0.5,\,0.5,\,0.5,\,0.5)^{\top}, \qquad \boldsymbol\phi_2 = (0.5,\,0.5,\,-0.5,\,-0.5)^{\top}$$ $$\hat\theta_1 = 3.0, \qquad \hat\theta_2 = -1.0$$
  1. Verify that both loading vectors are unit length and mutually orthogonal.
  2. Recover the implied coefficients $\hat\beta_j$ on the original predictors.

1. $\|\boldsymbol\phi_1\|^2 = 4(0.25) = 1$ and likewise $\|\boldsymbol\phi_2\|^2 = 1$, so both are unit length. Their inner product is $$0.25 + 0.25 - 0.25 - 0.25 = 0$$ so they are orthogonal, as principal component loadings must be.

2. Using $\hat\beta_j = \sum_m \hat\theta_m\phi_{jm}$: $$\hat\beta_1 = 3.0(0.5) + (-1.0)(0.5) = 1.5 - 0.5 = \mathbf{1.0}$$ $$\hat\beta_2 = 3.0(0.5) + (-1.0)(0.5) = \mathbf{1.0}$$ $$\hat\beta_3 = 3.0(0.5) + (-1.0)(-0.5) = 1.5 + 0.5 = \mathbf{2.0}$$ $$\hat\beta_4 = 3.0(0.5) + (-1.0)(-0.5) = \mathbf{2.0}$$ So $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (1.0,\,1.0,\,2.0,\,2.0)^{\top}$.

Practice Problems

Practice Problems

Problem 1 Four standardised predictors have covariances with the standardised response $(-0.6,\,0.3,\,0.0,\,0.9)$.
  1. Compute the first PLS direction.
  2. Which predictor dominates, and which is excluded?
  3. State how the answer would change if the response were multiplied by $10$.

1. $$\|\boldsymbol\phi\| = \sqrt{0.36+0.09+0.00+0.81} = \sqrt{1.26} = 1.1225$$ $$\boldsymbol\phi_1 = \frac{1}{1.1225}(-0.6,\,0.3,\,0.0,\,0.9)^{\top} = (-0.5345,\;0.2673,\;0.0000,\;0.8018)^{\top}$$ Check: $0.2857+0.0714+0+0.6429 = 1.0000$ ✓

2. $X_4$ dominates, with the largest weight in absolute value, and $X_1$ enters with a substantial negative weight. $X_3$ has zero covariance with $Y$, so it receives zero weight and is excluded from the first direction. It may still enter later directions, since after orthogonalisation its residual can become correlated with $Y$.

3. Nothing changes. Multiplying $Y$ by $10$ multiplies every covariance by $10$, hence multiplies $\|\boldsymbol\phi\|$ by $10$, and the normalisation divides it out. The direction is invariant to the scale of $Y$; only the scale of the predictors matters, which is why they must be standardised.

Problem 2 A dataset has $n = 40$ observations and $p = 60$ predictors.
  1. Explain why ordinary least squares cannot be used.
  2. What is the largest number of principal components that can be computed?
  3. Explain how PCR makes the problem tractable, and name one other method from this module that would also work.

1. With $p = 60 > n = 40$ the design matrix has more columns than rows, so $\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}$ is singular and cannot be inverted. Infinitely many coefficient vectors fit the data perfectly, giving training RSS of exactly zero and no unique solution. The fit is useless for prediction.

2. At most $\min(n-1,\,p) = \min(39,\,60) = \mathbf{39}$ components. Centring the data removes one degree of freedom, so $n-1$ rather than $n$.

3. PCR fits least squares of $Y$ on $M$ components where $M$ is chosen well below $n$, say $M = 5$. With $M + 1 = 6$ parameters and $40$ observations the regression is well determined, and the components are orthogonal so there is no collinearity. The high dimensional problem has been converted into a low dimensional one.

Alternative: PLS applies equally well, since it also regresses $Y$ on a small number of derived directions rather than on all $p$ original predictors.

Problem 3 Explain why PCR can perform worse than PLS even when both retain the same number of components, and describe a situation in which the two would give almost the same fit.

PCR worse. PCR is unsupervised, so its directions are chosen without reference to $Y$. If the response depends on a low variance direction of $X$, PCR discards it and no choice of $M$ below $p$ recovers the fit. PLS builds its directions using the covariance with $Y$, so it can pick up that direction in its first component or two.

Almost the same fit. When the directions of greatest variance in $X$ are also the directions most related to $Y$, the first PLS direction is close to the first principal component and the two methods agree. This is exactly the situation in which the assumption behind PCR holds.
