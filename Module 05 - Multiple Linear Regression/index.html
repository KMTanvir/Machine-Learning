Module 05: Multiple Linear Regression

Module 05

Multiple Linear Regression

Simple regression uses one predictor. In practice, many variables influence the response simultaneously. Multiple linear regression extends the model to $p$ predictors.

Multiple Linear Regression Model $$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p + \varepsilon$$ Each $\beta_j$ is the effect of $X_j$ on $Y$, holding all other predictors fixed.

Learning Objectives

  • Write the multiple regression model in matrix form
  • Interpret coefficients correctly in the presence of other predictors
  • Understand multicollinearity and its consequences
  • Perform and interpret the overall $F$-test
  • Approach variable selection
Motivation

Why Multiple Predictors?

Consider the Advertising data with three media: TV, Radio, Newspaper.

Problem with separate simple regressions: Running three separate regressions ignores correlations among predictors and gives misleading estimates. For example, Newspaper may appear significant because it is correlated with Radio, which is the truly effective medium.
Multiple regression solution: Fit all predictors together. Each coefficient measures the effect of that predictor, controlling for the others: $$\widehat{\text{Sales}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{TV} + \beta_2\,\text{Radio} + \beta_3\,\text{Newspaper}$$
In multiple regression, the coefficient on Newspaper becomes near zero and insignificant once Radio is included, revealing that Newspaper has little independent effect.
Matrix Form

Matrix Formulation

With $n$ observations and $p$ predictors, stack the data into matrices:

$$\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$$ where: $$\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix}y_1\\\vdots\\y_n\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1p}\\\vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\1 & x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{np}\end{pmatrix},\quad \boldsymbol{\beta} = \begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_1\\\vdots\\\beta_p\end{pmatrix}$$ $\mathbf{X}$ is $n \times (p+1)$; the first column of ones handles the intercept.
The matrix form is compact and enables powerful linear algebra. All theoretical results in regression derive from this formulation.
Estimation

OLS Estimation in Matrix Form

Minimise $\mathrm{RSS} = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 = (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})$.

OLS estimator (normal equations): $$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$$ provided $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is invertible (i.e., no perfect multicollinearity).
Fitted values: $$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}$$ where $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top$ is the hat matrix.
Residuals: $$\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}} = (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}$$
The hat matrix $\mathbf{H}$ projects $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space of $\mathbf{X}$. The diagonal entries $h_{ii}$ are the leverage values.
Interpretation

Interpreting Coefficients

Coefficient $\hat\beta_j$: The estimated average change in $Y$ associated with a one-unit increase in $X_j$, holding all other predictors constant.

Newspaper: Significant in simple regression (due to correlation with Radio), but insignificant once Radio is included. Multiple regression reveals the true picture.
Coefficient signs and magnitudes can change substantially when other predictors are added. Always fit all predictors together rather than separately.
Inference

The Overall F-Test

Are any of the predictors useful?

Hypotheses: $H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \cdots = \beta_p = 0$ vs $H_a:$ at least one $\beta_j \neq 0$
F-statistic: $$F = \frac{(\mathrm{TSS} - \mathrm{RSS})/p}{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p-1)} = \frac{\mathrm{ESS}/p}{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p-1)}$$ Under $H_0$: $F \sim F_{p,\,n-p-1}$
  • A large $F$ (and small $p$-value) gives evidence that at least one predictor is useful
  • If $n$ is large, even small $F$ can have low $p$-value: use judgment
  • Individual $t$-tests check each $\beta_j$ given the others are in the model
Do not skip the $F$-test: With many predictors, individual $t$-tests can be significant by chance alone (multiple testing problem). The $F$-test provides an overall guard.
Collinearity

Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity: Two or more predictors are highly correlated with each other. This makes it difficult to estimate individual coefficients precisely.

Consequences

  • Standard errors of affected coefficients become very large
  • $t$-statistics become small, even when the group of correlated predictors is collectively important
  • Estimates become unstable: small changes in data lead to large changes in $\hat\beta$
  • Interpretation of individual coefficients becomes ambiguous
Variance Inflation Factor (VIF): $$\mathrm{VIF}(\hat\beta_j) = \frac{1}{1 - R^2_{X_j|X_{-j}}}$$ where $R^2_{X_j|X_{-j}}$ is the $R^2$ from regressing $X_j$ on all other predictors. Rule of thumb: VIF $> 5$ or $10$ indicates problematic collinearity.
Remedies: Drop one of the collinear variables, combine them (e.g., average), or use regularisation (Ridge regression, covered later).
Variable Selection

Which Predictors to Include?

With $p$ predictors, there are $2^p$ possible subsets. We need principled selection strategies.

Forward Selection: Start with the null model. Repeatedly add the predictor that most improves fit (lowest $p$-value or largest reduction in RSS/AIC), until a stopping criterion is met.
Backward Elimination: Start with all $p$ predictors. Repeatedly remove the least significant predictor (largest $p$-value), until all remaining predictors are significant.
Stepwise Selection: Combines forward and backward: at each step, a variable may be added or removed.
Caution: These greedy procedures do not guarantee finding the globally best model. They can overfit if stopping criteria are based on $p$-values alone. Information criteria (AIC, BIC) or cross-validation are preferred for model selection.
Model Selection

Information Criteria for Model Selection

Adding predictors always reduces RSS, so we need criteria that penalise complexity.

AIC (Akaike Information Criterion): $$\mathrm{AIC} = n\log\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n}\right) + 2(p+1)$$ BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion): $$\mathrm{BIC} = n\log\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n}\right) + (p+1)\log n$$ Adjusted $R^2$: $$\bar R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p-1)}{\mathrm{TSS}/(n-1)}$$
  • Lower AIC/BIC is better. BIC penalises complexity more heavily than AIC for $n > 7$.
  • Adjusted $R^2$ penalises adding useless predictors; higher is better.
  • Cross-validation directly estimates test error (most principled, covered in Module 10).
Extensions

Qualitative Predictors and Interactions

Dummy (indicator) variables: For a qualitative predictor with $k$ levels, create $k-1$ dummy variables. Example: gender (Male/Female) becomes one dummy $D = 1$ if Female, $0$ if Male. $$\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 D + \hat\beta_2 X$$
Interaction terms: When the effect of $X_1$ on $Y$ depends on the value of $X_2$, include the product $X_1 \cdot X_2$: $$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 X_1 X_2 + \varepsilon$$ The slope of $X_1$ is now $\beta_1 + \beta_3 X_2$.
Hierarchical principle: If an interaction term $X_1 X_2$ is included, always include the main effects $X_1$ and $X_2$ too, even if they are individually insignificant.
In-Class Exercise

In-Class Exercise: Multiple Regression Output

A regression of salary (in thousands of dollars) on years of experience ($X_1$) and education level (dummy: $X_2 = 1$ if postgraduate, 0 otherwise) gives:

Predictor$\hat\beta$SE$t$$p$-value
Intercept35.03.210.94<0.001
Experience ($X_1$)4.50.67.50<0.001
Postgrad ($X_2$)12.02.15.71<0.001

$n = 120$, $R^2 = 0.72$, $F = 150.3$ ($p < 0.001$), RSE $= 8.4$.

  1. Interpret $\hat\beta_1$ and $\hat\beta_2$ in context.
  2. Predict salary for an employee with 10 years of experience and a postgraduate degree.
  3. What does the $F$-statistic test? What do you conclude?
  4. Is there evidence of a multicollinearity problem? What additional information would you need?

1. $\hat\beta_1 = 4.5$: each additional year of experience is associated with an average salary increase of $4,500, holding education level constant. $\hat\beta_2 = 12.0$: postgraduate employees earn, on average, $12,000 more than non-postgraduate employees with the same experience.

2. $\hat y = 35.0 + 4.5(10) + 12.0(1) = 35 + 45 + 12 = \$92{,}000$

3. The $F$-test tests $H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = 0$. $F = 150.3$ with $p < 0.001$: strong evidence that at least one of experience or education level is a useful predictor of salary.

4. We cannot judge multicollinearity from this table alone. We would need the VIF values (or the correlation between $X_1$ and $X_2$). The SEs look reasonable, so severe multicollinearity is unlikely here.

Practice Problems

Practice Problems

Problem 1: Interpretation and Prediction

A real estate model: $\widehat{\mathrm{Price}} = 50 + 0.15\,\mathrm{Area} + 8\,\mathrm{Rooms} - 3\,\mathrm{Age}$, where Price is in thousands of dollars, Area in sq. ft., Rooms is number of bedrooms, Age in years.

  1. Interpret the coefficient on Age.
  2. Predict the price of a 1500 sq. ft., 3-bedroom house that is 10 years old.
  3. Which predictor has the largest individual effect? Is this comparison meaningful?

a. Holding area and rooms fixed, each additional year of age is associated with a 3,000 dollar decrease in price.

b. $\hat y=50+0.15(1500)+8(3)-3(10)=50+225+24-30=269$, i.e., 269 thousand dollars.

c. No: raw coefficient magnitudes depend on each predictor's scale. Standardised coefficients allow fair comparison of effect sizes.

Problem 2: F-test

A regression with $p=4$ predictors, $n=100$ observations gives $\mathrm{TSS} = 5000$, $\mathrm{RSS} = 2000$.

  1. Compute the $F$-statistic for the overall test.
  2. State the null hypothesis and interpret the result.
  3. Compute adjusted $R^2$.

a. $F=\frac{(5000-2000)/4}{2000/(100-4-1)}=\frac{750}{21.05}\approx35.6$.

b. $H_0:\beta_1=\cdots=\beta_4=0$ (no predictors are useful). $F\approx35.6$ with $df=(4,95)$: very strong evidence against $H_0$; at least one predictor is useful.

c. $\bar R^2=1-(2000/95)/(5000/99)=1-0.417=0.583$.

Problem 3: Multicollinearity

A researcher fits a model with $X_1$ (income) and $X_2$ (wealth) to predict spending. She gets $\hat\beta_1 = 0.3$ ($p = 0.42$) and $\hat\beta_2 = 0.2$ ($p = 0.51$), but $F = 45$ ($p < 0.001$) and $R^2 = 0.80$.

  1. Is this pattern surprising? Explain what might be causing it.
  2. How would you diagnose this more formally?
  3. What is one possible remedy?

a. Likely multicollinearity: income and wealth are highly correlated, inflating standard errors so neither $t$-test is individually significant despite the model having strong joint predictive power.

b. Compute the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for each predictor; VIF $>10$ signals severe multicollinearity.

c. Possible remedies: remove one predictor, form a composite, or use ridge regression.

Problem 4: Interaction Terms

A fitted model is: $\hat Y = 10 + 3X_1 + 5X_2 - 2X_1X_2$, where $X_2 \in \{0,1\}$ is a group indicator.

  1. What is the slope of $X_1$ for group $X_2 = 0$? For group $X_2 = 1$?
  2. In which group does $X_1$ have a larger effect on $Y$?
  3. At what value of $X_2$ would the two groups have the same predicted $Y$ when $X_1 = 4$?

a. $X_2=0$: slope of $X_1$ is $3-2(0)=3$. $X_2=1$: slope is $3-2(1)=1$.

b. Group $X_2=0$ has a larger $X_1$ effect (slope 3 vs 1).

c. Set $10+3X_1=15+X_1\Rightarrow 2X_1=5\Rightarrow X_1=2.5$. The two groups predict the same $Y$ when $X_1=2.5$.

Summary

Module 05 Summary

Key Formulas
  • Model: $Y = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$
  • OLS: $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$
  • $F = \frac{\mathrm{ESS}/p}{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p-1)}$
  • $\bar R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p-1)}{\mathrm{TSS}/(n-1)}$
  • $\mathrm{VIF}_j = 1/(1-R^2_{X_j|X_{-j}})$
Key Ideas
  • Coefficients are partial effects (all others held fixed)
  • $F$-test: is any predictor useful?
  • Multicollinearity inflates SEs, check VIF
  • Use AIC/BIC/adj. $R^2$ for model selection
  • Include main effects when using interactions
