Module 24

Hierarchical Clustering

K-means requires the number of clusters to be fixed in advance and returns a single flat partition. Hierarchical clustering instead builds a whole family of nested clusterings at once, displayed as a tree, and leaves the choice of how many groups to take until after the structure has been seen.

Learning Objectives
  • Execute agglomerative clustering by hand from a distance matrix
  • Apply complete, single, average and centroid linkage, and explain how they differ
  • Read a dendrogram correctly, including what its horizontal axis does not mean
  • Choose between Euclidean and correlation based dissimilarity
  • Compare hierarchical clustering with K-means and state when each is preferable
The nesting property The clusterings produced are nested: the two group solution is obtained by merging two of the three groups, and so on. This is a genuine assumption about the data. If the natural split by sex cuts across the natural split by nationality, no hierarchy can represent both, and the method will impose one at the expense of the other.
Algorithm

Agglomerative Clustering and the Dendrogram

The algorithm, bottom up
  1. Begin with $n$ clusters, each containing one observation, and compute all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairwise dissimilarities.
  2. Find the two closest clusters and merge them. Record the height at which they joined.
  3. Compute the dissimilarity between the new cluster and each remaining cluster, using the chosen linkage.
  4. Repeat until one cluster remains.
The procedure performs exactly $n-1$ merges and requires no choice of $K$ in advance.

Reading a dendrogram

Each merge is drawn as a horizontal join, placed at a height equal to the dissimilarity at which it occurred. The height is the only thing that carries meaning.

Divisive clustering The opposite strategy starts with one cluster containing everything and recursively splits it. Divisive methods are far less commonly used, since the first split requires searching an enormous number of possible partitions.
Linkage

Linkage Methods

Once clusters contain more than one observation, the distance between two clusters must be defined from the distances between their members. That definition is the linkage.

The four standard choices

Step 1, height 1 The smallest entry is $1$, occurring twice: $(A,B)$ and $(D,E)$. Merge $\{A,B\}$, then $\{D,E\}$, both at height $1$. Step 2, updating with complete linkage Take the maximum across members: $$d(\{A,B\},C) = \max(4,3) = 4, \qquad d(C,\{D,E\}) = \max(4,5) = 5$$ $$d(\{A,B\},\{D,E\}) = \max(8,9,7,8) = 9$$ Step 3, height 4 The smallest remaining is $4$, so $C$ joins $\{A,B\}$ to give $\{A,B,C\}$ at height $4$. Step 4, height 9 Only two clusters remain and $$d(\{A,B,C\},\{D,E\}) = \max(8,9,7,8,4,5) = 9$$ so the final merge occurs at height $9$.

Dendrogram for complete linkage on the five observations.

Merge heights $$1,\quad 1,\quad 4,\quad 9$$ The large gap between the third merge at $4$ and the final merge at $9$ is the visual signal that two well separated groups are present. Cutting anywhere between those heights yields $\{A,B,C\}$ and $\{D,E\}$.
Worked Example

Worked Example: Single Linkage on the Same Data

Repeating the calculation with single linkage changes only the update rule: take the minimum across members rather than the maximum.

The calculation Steps 1 and 2 are unchanged, since the smallest entries are the same: $\{A,B\}$ and $\{D,E\}$ both merge at height $1$. The updates now give $$d(\{A,B\},C) = \min(4,3) = 3, \qquad d(C,\{D,E\}) = \min(4,5) = 4$$ $$d(\{A,B\},\{D,E\}) = \min(8,9,7,8) = 7$$ The smallest is $3$, so $C$ joins $\{A,B\}$ at height $\mathbf{3}$, not $4$. Finally $$d(\{A,B,C\},\{D,E\}) = \min(8,9,7,8,4,5) = 4$$ so the last merge occurs at height $\mathbf{4}$, not $9$.

Dendrogram for single linkage on the same five observations.

What this shows The clustering is identical; only the heights differ. Both linkages recover $\{A,B,C\}$ and $\{D,E\}$, because the structure here is unambiguous. But complete linkage separates the final merge from the previous one by a gap of $9-4 = 5$, while single linkage leaves a gap of only $4-3 = 1$. The two group structure is therefore visually obvious under complete linkage and easy to miss under single linkage. The reason is that single linkage measures the distance between the nearest members, $C$ and $D$, which are only $4$ apart, whereas complete linkage measures the distance between the furthest members, $A$ and $E$, which are $9$ apart. Linkage choice can change which structures are visible even when it does not change the groups.
Dissimilarity

Choosing the Dissimilarity Measure

Euclidean distance $$d(x_i,x_{i'}) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}(x_{ij}-x_{i'j})^2}$$ Two observations are similar when their values are close in magnitude. This is the default and the right choice when the actual levels matter.
Correlation based dissimilarity $$d(x_i,x_{i'}) = 1 - \operatorname{corr}(x_i,x_{i'})$$ Here the correlation is computed across the variables for a pair of observations, so two observations are similar when their profiles move together, regardless of level.

Why the choice can reverse the answer

Consider a retailer clustering shoppers by quantities purchased across many items. Under Euclidean distance, an infrequent shopper who buys little of everything is close to other infrequent shoppers, so clusters correspond largely to total spend. Under correlation dissimilarity, that shopper is grouped with heavy spenders who buy the same mix of items, so clusters correspond to taste rather than volume. Both are defensible; they answer different questions, and the choice must be made on substantive grounds.

Standardisation is a third decision Whether to standardise the variables is separate from the choice of dissimilarity, and equally consequential. Unstandardised, a variable measured in large units dominates the distance. In the retail example, standardising each item by its standard deviation gives rare purchases much more influence, which may be desirable if buying an unusual item is highly informative. There is no default that is always correct, so the decision should be stated explicitly.
Cutting the Tree

From Dendrogram to Clusters

Cutting A horizontal cut across the dendrogram at height $h$ produces a clustering: each branch severed by the cut becomes one cluster. Lowering the cut produces more, smaller clusters. A single tree therefore contains every clustering from $n$ singletons to one group, which is the practical advantage over K-means.

Where to cut

  • The largest vertical gap. A long stretch with no merges means clusters had to become much more dissimilar before joining, which is the clearest evidence of separation. In the worked example the gap from $4$ to $9$ points to a cut giving two clusters.
  • A target number of clusters, when the application dictates one, for example a fixed number of market segments.
  • The silhouette coefficient from Module 23, computed at each candidate cut and maximised.
The cut is not a test As with the elbow in K-means, this is a judgement supported by a diagnostic, not an inference. A dendrogram can always be cut, and will always yield clusters, whether or not the data contain any. Before reporting, check that the structure survives resampling: cluster a random subsample and see whether the same groups appear.
Comparison

Hierarchical Clustering Compared with K-Means

DeterminismDepends on initialisationDeterministic given the inputs

Practical guidance Use hierarchical clustering for moderate $n$ when the number of groups is unknown, when the tree itself is informative, or when a deterministic and reproducible result is required. Use K-means for large $n$, when $K$ is known or can be justified, and when clusters are expected to be roughly spherical. Running both and comparing is inexpensive and often more informative than either alone: agreement between two methods with quite different assumptions is meaningful evidence that the structure is real.
The irrevocability of merges Hierarchical clustering never reconsiders. An early merge made on a small distance persists to the top of the tree even if later evidence suggests it was wrong. K-means, by contrast, allows an observation to move at any iteration. This is the same greedy limitation seen in tree growing in Module 18.

Practice Problems

Practice Problems

Problem 1 A dendrogram built with average linkage has merge heights $0.8,\;1.1,\;1.3,\;3.9,\;4.2$.
  1. How many observations were there, and how many merges?
  2. Where would you cut, and how many clusters does that give?
  3. What would cutting at height $1.2$ give?

1. There are five heights, and agglomerative clustering performs $n-1$ merges, so $n = \mathbf{6}$ observations and $\mathbf{5}$ merges.

2. The gaps between consecutive heights are $0.3,\;0.2,\;2.6,\;0.3$. The largest by far is the jump from $1.3$ to $3.9$, so cut anywhere in that range, for example at $2.5$. The first three merges lie below the cut and the last two above it, so the cut severs $\mathbf{3}$ branches and gives three clusters.

3. Cutting at $1.2$ leaves the merges at $0.8$ and $1.1$ below the cut, and the merges at $1.3$, $3.9$ and $4.2$ above it. That gives $\mathbf{4}$ clusters. Since $1.2$ sits inside a run of closely spaced merges, this cut splits groups that the data barely distinguish, and is harder to defend than the cut in part 2.

Problem 2 Four observations have the following distance matrix.
$P$$Q$$R$$S$
$P$02610
$Q$048
$R$03
$S$0
  1. Perform average linkage clustering, giving every merge and its height.
  2. Cut the dendrogram at height $4$. What clusters result?
  3. Find the height of the final merge under complete linkage and under single linkage, and compare with average linkage.

1. The smallest entry is $d(P,Q)=2$, so merge $\{P,Q\}$ at height $\mathbf{2}$. Average linkage uses the mean of all pairwise distances: $$d(\{P,Q\},R)=\frac{6+4}{2}=5, \qquad d(\{P,Q\},S)=\frac{10+8}{2}=9, \qquad d(R,S)=3$$ The smallest is $3$, so merge $\{R,S\}$ at height $\mathbf{3}$. Finally $$d(\{P,Q\},\{R,S\})=\frac{6+10+4+8}{4}=7$$ so the last merge is at height $\mathbf{7}$.

2. The merges at $2$ and $3$ lie below the cut and the merge at $7$ lies above it, so the cut gives $2$ clusters: $\{P,Q\}$ and $\{R,S\}$.

3. Using the same four pairwise distances between $\{P,Q\}$ and $\{R,S\}$, namely $6,10,4,8$: $$\text{complete: } \max = 10, \qquad \text{single: } \min = 4, \qquad \text{average: } \tfrac{28}{4} = 7$$ Average linkage always lies between single and complete linkage, which is why it is described as a compromise.

Problem 3 Three patients are measured on age (years) and income (thousand taka): $P_1=(30,\,40)$, $P_2=(40,\,42)$, $P_3=(31,\,70)$. The standard deviations are $5$ for age and $20$ for income.
  1. Compute the three Euclidean distances on the raw measurements. Which pair is closest?
  2. Standardise each variable by dividing by its standard deviation, and recompute the distances. Which pair is closest now?
  3. Explain the difference.

1. Raw measurements. $$d(P_1,P_2)=\sqrt{10^2+2^2}=\sqrt{104}=10.20$$ $$d(P_1,P_3)=\sqrt{1^2+30^2}=\sqrt{901}=30.02, \qquad d(P_2,P_3)=\sqrt{9^2+28^2}=\sqrt{865}=29.41$$ The closest pair is $(P_1,P_2)$.

2. After dividing by the standard deviations the points become $P_1=(6,\,2)$, $P_2=(8,\,2.1)$, $P_3=(6.2,\,3.5)$. $$d(P_1,P_2)=\sqrt{2^2+0.1^2}=\sqrt{4.01}=2.00$$ $$d(P_1,P_3)=\sqrt{0.2^2+1.5^2}=\sqrt{2.29}=1.51, \qquad d(P_2,P_3)=\sqrt{1.8^2+1.4^2}=\sqrt{5.20}=2.28$$ The closest pair is now $(P_1,P_3)$.

3. On the raw scale, income varies over a much wider range than age, so the income difference dominates every distance and the age difference barely matters. $P_1$ and $P_3$ are the same age but far apart in income, so they look distant. After standardising, one standard deviation of age counts the same as one standard deviation of income, and $P_1$ and $P_3$, almost identical in age, become the closest pair. The clustering therefore depends on the scaling, which is why variables should be standardised before computing distances.
