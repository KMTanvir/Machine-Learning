Module 04: Simple Linear Regression

Module 04

Simple Linear Regression

We begin Part B: Linear Models. Linear regression is the foundation of supervised statistical learning, combining interpretability with practical effectiveness.

Simple Linear Regression Models the relationship between a single predictor $X$ and a response $Y$ as a straight line: $$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon$$

Learning Objectives

  • Write and interpret the simple linear model
  • Derive least squares estimates $\hat\beta_0$, $\hat\beta_1$
  • Compute standard errors, $t$-tests, and confidence intervals
  • Assess model fit using RSE and $R^2$
Reference: ISLR Chapter 3
Motivation

Why Linear Regression?

Given data $(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)$, we want to understand how $Y$ relates to $X$ and predict new $Y$ values.

Questions answered:
  • Is there a relationship between $X$ and $Y$?
  • How strong and in which direction?
  • How accurately can we predict $Y$?
  • Is the relationship linear?
Example: Advertising data
  • $X$: TV budget ($1000s)
  • $Y$: Sales (1000 units)
  • $n = 200$ markets
  • Goal: predict sales from budget
The population regression line: $$f(X) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$$ We never know $\beta_0, \beta_1$; we estimate them from data.
Model

The Simple Linear Model

Model: $Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon$
  • $\beta_0$: intercept, expected value of $Y$ when $X=0$
  • $\beta_1$: slope, average change in $Y$ per unit increase in $X$
  • $\varepsilon$: random error, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$

Key Assumptions

  • Linearity: $E[Y|X] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$
  • Independence: errors $\varepsilon_i$ are independent
  • Constant variance: $\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2$ for all $i$
  • Normality: $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ (needed for exact inference)
For large $n$, the normality assumption is less critical: the CLT ensures that $\hat\beta_1$ is approximately normal regardless.
Estimation

Least Squares Estimation

Choose $\hat\beta_0$ and $\hat\beta_1$ to minimise the Residual Sum of Squares:

$$\mathrm{RSS} = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i)^2$$

Closed-Form Solution (Normal Equations)

$$\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}$$ $$\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x$$
Derivation sketch: Differentiate RSS with respect to $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$, set derivatives to zero, and solve the two resulting equations.
Estimation

Fitted Values and Residuals

Fitted values: $$\hat y_i = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_i$$
Residuals: $$e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$$
OLS properties:
  • $\sum e_i = 0$
  • $\sum x_i e_i = 0$
  • Fitted line passes through $(\bar x, \bar y)$
  • OLS is BLUE (Best Linear Unbiased Estimator) by Gauss-Markov theorem
Residuals are the vertical distances from data points to the fitted line. Minimising RSS is equivalent to minimising the sum of squared residuals.
Inference

Standard Errors

The estimates $\hat\beta_0$ and $\hat\beta_1$ vary across repeated samples. Standard errors quantify this variability.

$$\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0)^2 = \sigma^2\!\left[\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}\right]$$ Estimated using $\hat\sigma^2 = \dfrac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-2}$ (RSE squared).
Why $n-2$? Two parameters $(\beta_0,\beta_1)$ estimated, so 2 degrees of freedom are used.
Key insight: Larger $S_{xx}$ (more spread in $X$) gives smaller $\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)$. A well-spread design is more informative.
Inference

Hypothesis Testing: Is There a Relationship?

Hypotheses: $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$ vs $H_a: \beta_1 \neq 0$
Test statistic: $$t = \frac{\hat\beta_1}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)} \;\sim\; t_{n-2} \text{ under } H_0$$

Decision

  • $p\text{-value} = 2P(T_{n-2} \geq |t|)$
  • Reject $H_0$ if $p$-value $< \alpha$ (commonly $\alpha = 0.05$)
  • A small $p$-value: evidence that $X$ is useful for predicting $Y$
The same $t$-test applies to $\beta_0$, though testing $\beta_0 = 0$ is rarely of primary interest.
Inference

Confidence Intervals and Prediction

95% CI for $\beta_1$: $$\hat\beta_1 \pm t_{n-2,\,0.025} \cdot \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1) \approx \hat\beta_1 \pm 1.96\cdot\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)$$
Confidence interval for mean at $x^*$: $$\hat y^* \pm t\cdot\mathrm{SE}(\hat\mu)$$ Covers the average response. Narrower.
Prediction interval for new observation at $x^*$: $$\hat y^* \pm t\cdot\mathrm{SE}(\hat y)$$ Covers a single future value. Always wider.
Key: $\mathrm{SE}(\hat y)^2 = \mathrm{SE}(\hat\mu)^2 + \hat\sigma^2$. The extra $\hat\sigma^2$ accounts for irreducible variability in a new observation.
Model Fit

Assessing Fit: RSE and R²

Residual Standard Error: $$\mathrm{RSE} = \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-2}}$$ Average deviation from fitted line. Same units as $Y$.
R-squared: $$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}}$$ $\mathrm{TSS} = \sum(y_i - \bar y)^2$ (total variance)

Variance Decomposition

$$\underbrace{\mathrm{TSS}}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\mathrm{ESS}}_{\text{explained}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{RSS}}_{\text{residual}}$$
  • $R^2 \in [0,1]$: proportion of variance in $Y$ explained by $X$
  • For simple regression: $R^2 = r^2$ (square of Pearson correlation)
Caution: High $R^2$ does not imply causation or that the model is correctly specified.
Diagnostics

Model Diagnostics: LINE Assumptions

LINE: the four assumptions of the linear model
  • Linearity: $E[Y|X] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$: the mean response is a straight line in $X$
  • Independence: errors $\varepsilon_i$ are independent across observations
  • Normality: $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, required for exact $t$-tests and CIs
  • Equal variance (homoscedasticity): $\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2$ constant for all $i$

Practice Problems

Practice Problems

Problem 1: Interpretation

Regression of house price (in thousands of dollars) on floor area (sq. ft): $\widehat{\mathrm{Price}} = 40.5 + 0.18 \times \mathrm{Area}$, $\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = 0.021$, $n = 80$.

  1. Interpret $\hat\beta_0$ and $\hat\beta_1$.
  2. Test $H_0:\beta_1=0$ at the 5% level. State your conclusion.
  3. Construct a 95% CI for $\beta_1$.

a. $\hat\beta_0=40.5$: predicted price (in thousands) when area$=0$ (not meaningful on its own). $\hat\beta_1=0.18$: each extra sq. ft. is associated with a 0.18 thousand dollar increase in price, holding other variables fixed.

b. $t=0.18/0.021\approx8.57$. With $df=78$, $t^*\approx1.99$. Since $8.57\gg1.99$, reject $H_0$: strong evidence that floor area predicts price.

c. $0.18\pm1.99\times0.021=(0.138,\ 0.222)$.

Problem 2: R² and RSE

For $n=25$: $\mathrm{TSS}=4800$, $\mathrm{RSS}=1200$, $\hat\beta_1=3.2$, $\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=0.8$.

  1. Compute $R^2$ and interpret.
  2. Compute the RSE.
  3. Is there evidence of a linear relationship at $\alpha=0.01$?

a. $R^2=1-1200/4800=0.75$. The model explains 75% of variation in $Y$.

b. RSE$=\sqrt{1200/(25-2)}=\sqrt{1200/23}\approx7.22$.

c. $t=3.2/0.8=4.0$, $df=23$, $t^*\approx2.81$ at $\alpha=0.01$. Since $4.0>2.81$, reject $H_0$: evidence of a linear relationship.

Problem 3: Confidence vs Prediction Interval

At $x^*=10$: $\hat y^*=32.5$, $\mathrm{SE}(\hat\mu)=1.8$, $\hat\sigma=5.6$.

  1. 95% CI for $E[Y|X=10]$.
  2. 95% PI for a new observation at $X=10$.
  3. Why is the PI wider?

a. 95% CI: $32.5\pm1.96\times1.8=(28.9,\ 36.1)$.

b. SE(new)$=\sqrt{1.8^2+5.6^2}=\sqrt{34.6}\approx5.88$. 95% PI: $32.5\pm1.96\times5.88=(21.0,\ 44.0)$.

c. The PI is wider because it accounts for both the uncertainty in $\hat\mu$ and the irreducible noise $\sigma^2$ of a new observation.

Problem 4: True or False
  1. If $R^2=0.95$, the model predicts any new observation to within 5% of its true value.
  2. In simple linear regression, $R^2 = r^2$ (square of Pearson correlation).
  3. A large $p$-value for $\hat\beta_1$ proves there is no relationship between $X$ and $Y$.
  4. A prediction interval is always at least as wide as the corresponding confidence interval.

a. False. $R^2$ is a proportion of variance explained, not a bound on individual prediction errors.

b. True. In simple linear regression, $R^2=r^2$ exactly.

c. False. A large $p$-value is consistent with $H_0$ but does not prove $\beta_1=0$.

d. True. The PI adds $\hat\sigma^2$ for the new observation, so PI is always at least as wide as the CI.

Summary

Module 04 Summary

Key Formulas
  • $\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}$
  • $\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1\bar x$
  • $\mathrm{RSE} = \sqrt{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-2)}$
  • $R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS}$
  • $t = \hat\beta_1/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)$
Key Ideas
  • OLS minimises RSS
  • OLS is BLUE (Gauss-Markov)
  • $t$-test checks if $X$ is useful
  • CI: uncertainty about mean; PI: uncertainty about new obs.
  • Always check residual plots
